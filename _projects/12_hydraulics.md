I want to teach my two kids, ages 10 and 11, real STEM from first principles, and I want to test whether a short, hands-on module in the garage can do that better than a worksheet. Hydraulics is the first test. It is physical and a bit messy, and a kid can feel the physics in their hands: push one syringe and something across the table lifts more than you pushed.

### What it is

**Garage Hydraulics** is a six-week module, one session a week, for ages 8–13. It ends with every team driving a **four-joint hydraulic robotic arm** they built themselves: base rotation, shoulder, elbow and gripper, all powered by water-filled syringes and run from a control panel.

The arm isn't a kit to follow step by step. Every week introduces one idea, tests it on the bench, and then builds it into the arm:

- **Levers.** Trading force for distance, and why an excavator is three levers stacked together.
- **Pascal's principle.** Why water works and air doesn't, and how a small push becomes a big lift.
- **Joints.** Turning a push into a swing, and choosing between reach and strength.
- **Grippers.** Grabbing an object without dropping it or crushing it.
- **System integration.** Putting the arm together on its base and running it.
- **Iteration.** Timed trials, finding the weakest link, changing one thing and measuring the result.

### The design premise

Kids this age won't CAD a robot from scratch in six hours, and I won't pretend they do. What they *do* get is real engineering choices: where a cylinder attaches, which size drives which joint, which gripper to use. Each choice is tested against a measured result. That is what engineering work mostly is: tradeoffs, tested.

The structural parts are 3D printed, and the rest is inexpensive material that's easy to find. Each session connects the bench work to real machines: brakes, excavators, prosthetic hands and the Canadarm on the Space Station.
