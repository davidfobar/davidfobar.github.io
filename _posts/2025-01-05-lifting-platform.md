### Before

Before the platform, the floor was rubber mats laid straight on the concrete slab. That works, up to a point, but it had three problems I wanted gone:

- **It was soft.** Rubber gives underfoot. For squats and pulls you want the floor to be rigid, so the force you put into it comes straight back rather than being soaked up by the mat.
- **It didn't protect the slab much.** A single layer of rubber over concrete takes the edge off a dropped bar, but the slab is still right there.
- **It was loud.** I train early in the morning. A deadlift set down on a mat over concrete rings through the slab and into the rest of the house. The platform had to quiet that down.

### Layout

The platform is **8' × 6'** overall, split into three strips:

| Zone                   | Size     | Surface                  |
| ---------------------- | -------- | ------------------------ |
| Center (lifter stands) | 4' × 6'  | Oak flooring             |
| Left lifting station   | 2' × 6'  | Eleiko Gym Floor Tile    |
| Right lifting station  | 2' × 6'  | Eleiko Gym Floor Tile    |

That's the standard platform layout, and there's a good reason for it: the lifter stands on the hard center and the plates land on the rubber at either side. The wood never takes a drop, and the rubber sits right where the impact happens.

### The stack

Under the surface layer are **two layers of 3/4" plywood**, for 1-1/2" of plywood in total. Every layer is bonded to the one below it with **PL Premium** construction adhesive: plywood to plywood, and oak to plywood.

I staggered the seams between the two plywood layers so no joint runs all the way through the stack. Where one sheet ends in the bottom layer, the top layer carries straight across it. That makes the whole platform behave like one thick slab of wood instead of a collection of panels, which matters for both jobs it does. A load on any one spot is spread over a large area of concrete, and a dropped bar has a lot of mass to move before it can move the slab.

I didn't screw the layers together. Each layer was **weighted** while the adhesive cured. With a polyurethane adhesive like PL Premium, the job of weight or clamps is to keep the surfaces in contact while it cures. Screws would have done the same thing and left a grid of fasteners in the deck.

### The oak center

The center strip is oak flooring glued down onto the plywood, then finished with **polyurethane**. The poly gives the oak a hard, wipeable surface that stands up to chalk and shoes and doesn't soak up sweat.

### The frame

The edge of a layered platform is its weak point. Exposed plywood edges chip, the corners get kicked, and the layers can start to delaminate from the outside in. So the whole platform is wrapped in a frame of **2" angle iron**, welded at the corners, painted black, and **screwed to the plywood**.

The frame covers the exposed edge of the stack, protects the corners, and ties the perimeter together into a single unit. Because it's fastened into the plywood, it moves with the platform instead of sliding around it.

I painted it up on jack stands in the garage, which kept it clear of the floor and made it easy to reach every face.

### The lifting stations

The two outer strips are **Eleiko Gym Floor Tiles**. These are the only surface on the platform meant to take a dropped bar, and they're the reason it's quiet. The rubber absorbs the impact, the plywood stack spreads what's left, and very little reaches the slab.

They're also the sacrificial layer. If a tile wears out or gets torn up, it can be replaced without touching the oak or the plywood under it.
