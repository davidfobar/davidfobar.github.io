 |  The Home Gym

A two-car garage turned powerlifting gym — a three-layer platform, a welded storage wall, and a logo burned into the oak.

A commercial gym rents you someone else’s compromises. The bar you want is in use, the platform is carpet over concrete, and the hours are whoever else’s. A garage solves all of that at once, but it hands you a different problem: a garage is a slab, a water heater, and a door, and none of it is designed to have 400 pounds dropped on it.

This is the build that turned one into a place to train — paint, a lifting platform, and a storage wall welded to fit the rack.

The platform

The platform is the only part of a home gym that is genuinely structural. Everything else is equipment you could put anywhere; the platform is what stands between a loaded barbell and a concrete slab that will happily crack.

It’s a conventional three-layer build, and each layer is doing one job:

  • Two layers of plywood, seams run perpendicular to each other. The bottom layer spreads load into the slab; the top layer bridges the seams below it so no joint in the deck runs all the way through. A single thick layer would be stiffer per dollar but would transmit an impact straight down in one place — two staggered layers distribute it.
  • A hardwood top through the center. This is the part you stand on, and it’s the reason a platform feels different from a mat: it’s rigid. A squat or a pull wants the floor to give back exactly what you put in, and plywood under rubber doesn’t do that. The hardwood also takes the logo (below), which rubber can’t.
  • Rubber on the lifting stations. The two outer strips are where plates land. They’re the sacrificial layer — cheap, replaceable, and the only thing that should ever take a drop.

The rack straddles the wooden center and sits with its feet on the rubber, so the deck stays clear and the whole assembly is one unit instead of a rack next to a platform.

Burning the logo

The hardwood center carries the Platypus Powerlifting mark, cut into the wood with a diode laser rather than painted or stenciled. Paint on a lifting surface is a wear item — it scuffs off under shoes and chalk within a season. A burn is in the wood, so it wears at the same rate the deck does.

The deck boards were laid up and engraved as an assembled panel, not one board at a time. That matters: the artwork crosses the plank seams, so the circle stays a circle and the letters don’t step where the boards meet. Engraving individually and assembling afterward would have needed the boards to land back in exactly the same order and position, to within a laser kerf.

The storage wall

Floor space is the scarce resource in a garage gym, and dumbbells and kettlebells are the worst offenders — they’re dense, they don’t stack, and a commercial rack for them has a footprint bigger than the weights themselves.

The answer was to stop using the floor. Two steel shelves, welded to length and hung between the rack’s uprights, put the entire dumbbell collection inside the rack’s footprint — space that was already committed and otherwise doing nothing. Adjustable dumbbells go on the top shelf where they’re at hand; the fixed hex set runs along the lower shelf in descending order; the kettlebells sit on the floor underneath, still inside the rack.

Buying this wasn’t really an option. Rack-mounted storage is sold per-rack and per-upright-spacing, and none of it is sized for a mixed set of adjustables and hex dumbbells. Welding it meant the shelves are exactly as deep as the heaviest dumbbell needs and exactly as long as the gap between uprights.

Paint

The walls are painted, which sounds like the least interesting decision here and is actually one of the more useful ones. Bare drywall in a garage reads as storage; a finished wall reads as a room you train in. The practical upside is light — a mid-grey wall bounces enough of it that the space is usable without the door open, which matters from November onward.

It also gives the Sorinex banner somewhere to hang that isn’t a stud.

What’s in it

   
Rack Power rack with plate storage on the uprights
Platform Three-layer: 2× plywood, hardwood center, rubber lifting stations
Bars Multiple barbells, stored vertically against the wall
Plates Bumper plates plus iron, on the rack horns
Dumbbells Adjustable pair + fixed hex set, on the welded shelves
Kettlebells Six, under the shelves inside the rack
Bench Adjustable, wheeled
Accessories Belt, straps, bands, chains, plyo box, rollers, crash pad
