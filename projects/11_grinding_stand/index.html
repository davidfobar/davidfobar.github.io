 |  Concrete Grinding Stand

A cast-concrete pedestal built for grinding telescope mirrors — and for everything else the shop throws at it.

Grinding a telescope mirror means hours of walking slow circles around a waist-high work surface, leaning your weight into a tool. Any stand that racks, creeps, or rocks steals energy out of every stroke and puts it somewhere other than the glass. The fix is mass and stiffness, and the cheapest source of both is concrete.

So I cast one. But a single-purpose 300-pound pedestal is a poor trade for garage floor space, so the design gives up nothing to generality: the top is a bolt-on steel plate rather than a cast-in surface, and a 3” steel pipe cast into the centerline turns the top of the column into a mounting socket instead of dead concrete.

Design

Three decisions do most of the work:

  • The top plate is bolted, not cast. A 12”×12” × ½” steel plate lands on four coupling nuts welded directly to the rebar cage. The nuts are embedded in the pour; the plate comes off with four bolts. If the top ever gets chewed up, or a job wants a different one, the concrete doesn’t care.
  • A 3” sch 40 socket is cast into the centerline. It’s sized to accept a trailer hub, which makes the stand a genuinely stable rotary table. The same socket doubles as a plain cylindrical joint for the grinding plate — no hardware, just drop it in. It runs about 6” down from the top plate rather than the full height of the column — I’d have taken it all the way if I’d had the pipe on hand, but 6” is far more engagement than either job asks for.
  • Three feet, not four. Three points define a plane, so the stand sits solid on a garage slab that is anything but flat, and levels with three adjustments instead of a fight.

Casting it upside down

The top plate is the one surface that has to be genuinely flat and genuinely square to the column, so the whole assembly was cast inverted: plate face-down on the floor of the form, coupling nuts welded to the rebar cage pointing up into it, the 10” Quiktube column stacked on top of that, and the hexagonal base form up at the top where it could be poured last. The plate’s bearing face is therefore cast against the form itself rather than troweled — it comes out as flat as the form was.

The cost is support. An inverted pour like this is top-heavy and unstable through the entire cure, and every one of those stacked joints wants to leak. Joints were sealed with PL Premium, and the corner brackets holding the hex base form together were 3D printed.

The rebar is a welded cage rather than tied — it carries the coupling nuts at one end and spreads into the hex base at the other, so the top plate’s anchorage is continuous with the base reinforcement. Mix was nothing exotic: standard bagged concrete.

Feet

Each foot is a hex bolt welded to a plate, with a hockey puck screwed to the plate as the contact face. Pucks are cheap, dense, grippy on concrete, and don’t mark the slab. The bolts thread into the base for leveling.

Grinding

The grinding plate is a large HDPE cutting board cut to a circle just big enough to cover the square top plate corner to corner — about 17” across, the diagonal of a 12” square. A turned hub on its underside drops straight into the 3” socket. That single cylindrical joint locates the plate and lets it spin freely, with no fasteners to loosen and nothing to interfere with the working surface.

The 1–24 index marks around the rim are there to keep rotation honest. Figuring depends on distributing strokes evenly around the mirror, and eyeballing “about a sixth of a turn” drifts over an hour of work — numbered stops make it something you can count instead of estimate.

Everything else it does

A heavy, level, three-point pedestal with a bolt-on steel top turns out to be useful well beyond mirror work. It has since served as:

  • a welding table — the ½” top plate takes heat and clamps without complaint, and the mass keeps it put
  • a base for a bike maintenance stand — a repair stand is only as steady as what it’s bolted to, and this doesn’t tip
  • a pedestal for a shop vise — bolted to the top plate, with 360° access around it

Specifications

   
Overall height ≈32” to the top plate
Column 10” diameter, cast in a Quiktube form
Base Regular hexagon, 12” sides (≈20.8” across flats, 24” across points)
Top plate 12” × 12” × ½” steel, bolted to four embedded coupling nuts
Central socket 3” sch 40 steel tube, ≈6” deep from the top plate
Reinforcement Welded rebar cage, continuous base to top plate
Concrete Standard bagged mix
Feet Three leveling feet — hex bolt welded to plate, hockey puck contact face
Grinding plate HDPE cutting board, ≈17” diameter, turned hub, 24-position index
Weight ≈300 lbs
